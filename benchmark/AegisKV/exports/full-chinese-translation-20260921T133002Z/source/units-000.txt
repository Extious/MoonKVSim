U000 heading 
摘要

U001 prose 
Multi-agent applications accumulate reusable context while executing
interdependent LLM requests. A worker failure can lose this state and delay
several branches at once, yet protecting every context in fast memory is
expensive. We propose AegisKV, a workflow-aware manager for KV checkpoints
across Peer HBM, shared DRAM and shared storage. A complete checkpoint
spans the three tiers, with one managed owner per object: selected layers
and hot blocks reside on a preassigned takeover DP, and lower tiers
retain the rest. Request slack and opportunity-normalized block demand
provide a factorized recovery priority; normal reuse contributes independent
retention value. Renewable per-Agent credit manages DRAM and storage,
with lazy refresh separating demand updates from actual-use renewal.
Peer uses fixed layer/block quotas and greedy zero-slack admission.
An AIMD Adapter bounds total Peer capacity, while versioned transfers
preserve checkpoint coverage during migration and explicit fallback.
These bounded policies preserve native attention semantics. We connect trace-derived
workflow observations and existing reconstruction microbenchmarks to a
reproducible analytical study and a complete experimental protocol.
In 36 declared Peer-disabled scenarios, the placement model improves on
LRU in 17 and ties in 19. Matched analytical schedules illustrate Peer's
incremental overlap benefit; neither model measures a three-tier system. Historical outage timing drives a separate recovery-load model in which
a lower mean coexists with worse tail latency. Mechanism ablations expose
interactions between layer placement and overlap, and a negative result
for an earlier rental screen. Bounded repairs reduce that model's gap
but do not establish the new controller's performance. The study
identifies when contention, prediction error, and commit
lag limit the benefit. End-to-end fault injection and normal
service measurements remain necessary to establish the proposed system's
performance.

U002 heading 1
Introduction

U004 prose 
A multi-agent application completes when its required results are ready:
a tested patch, a report, or an answer assembled from parallel searches.
An LLM request's value therefore depends on its workflow position. A
critical-branch delay postpones completion; another branch may absorb it
before a join. Application-aware serving exploits this structure during
normal execution~\cite{lin2024parrot,pan2025kvflow,ni2026topas}. Failures
raise a further question: which histories should remain quickly
recoverable when one worker interrupts several requests together?

U005 prose 
Long contexts make that choice expensive. Agents accumulate reasoning,
code, and tool results; losing their GPU-resident KV can require substantial
reconstruction. Checkpointing avoids that work~\cite{strati2024dejavu,jayakody2026ghostserve},
and load-aware recovery distributes interrupted requests~\cite{cao2026lumen}.
Hierarchical caches provide DRAM and storage capacity~\cite{qin2025mooncake,xie2026strata},
but keeping every history in the faster tier is costly.

U006 prose 
Recency and recovery latency alone do not determine placement. A long,
recent history may belong to a branch that tolerates storage access,
while a smaller history lies on the critical path. High slack does not
justify deleting reusable history: subsequent normal calls may repeatedly
reconstruct it. Common-worker failures also couple requests through the
DAG and shared recovery resources. Accelerating only one of two delayed
branches may leave their join unchanged. Finally, demotion is asynchronous:
freeing DRAM before its storage replacement commits invalidates protection.

U007 prose 
AegisKV manages checkpoints across Peer HBM, shared DRAM and shared storage.
Its Tracker estimates remaining workflow time; the Master combines
request urgency with opportunity-normalized block demand. Distinct Agents
contribute credit to shared objects, while one Agent's actual reuse renews
its own share. Lower-tier rent ages byte occupancy, and lazy value updates
preserve paid rent. Peer uses fixed layer/block quotas for zero-slack
requests; its hot identities and admitted requests change online.
Objects have one managed owner after migration. A bounded event loop
connects retention, admission and cascading demotion. The Router binds a
compatible takeover DP at arrival; an AIMD Adapter bounds total Peer
capacity and Connector executes transfers.
Versioned manifests, reservations and reader leases preserve checkpoint
coverage as ownership moves between tiers.

U008 prose 
Our proposed contributions are a workflow-aware priority and renewable-credit
policy that separates Peer acceleration from lower-tier retention; a bounded
online controller and safe transition
protocol; and reproducible analytical scenarios with a matched physical
experiment plan. The model exposes gains, ties, prediction sensitivity,
and regret against a feasible oracle. Trace-driven recovery and independent
mechanism models expose queue-tail regressions and an adverse historical
rental screen, whose bounded repairs leave a residual gap. These results
motivate and constrain the proposed
system; they do not measure its implementation. The central empirical
hypothesis is that workflow-aware protection improves finite-resource
allocation after accounting for healthy-service and migration costs.

U009 heading 2
Background and Motivation

U011 heading 2.1
Background

U013 heading 
Multi-agent execution.

U014 prose 
A multi-agent application divides a user task among agents that exchange
intermediate results. Each agent maintains a conversation and advances
through LLM requests and tool calls: the model generates a response or
a tool invocation, and the returned result becomes input to a subsequent
request~\cite{wu2023autogen}. A \emph{request} is one model invocation;
an \emph{agent} can issue multiple requests to complete its task; a
\emph{workflow} connects these tasks through sequential dependencies,
parallel branches and joins. The application orchestrates this workflow,
while the serving engine executes its model requests.

U015 prose 
Figure~\ref{fig:agent-workflow-examples} illustrates this structure.
For code generation, a main agent delegates a CSV-export feature to API,
UI, test-authoring and documentation agents. Starting from a shared
specification, each branch alternates model requests with file reads,
edits or checks. The main agent then integrates the changes and runs
tests. For deep research, a lead agent delegates technical, cost, safety
and supply investigations, then synthesizes their evidence into a
report~\cite{anthropic2025multiagent}. Branches can require different
numbers of requests and tools; there is no common round barrier.
A completed model request can therefore leave both its agent and the
workflow unfinished.

U016 heading 
Workflow completion time.

U017 prose 
The user receives a result only after the workflow produces its required
outputs. We measure this interval as \emph{workflow completion time}
(WCT): for workflow $w$ released at $a_w$ and completed at $c_w$,
$\mathrm{WCT}_w=c_w-a_w$. It includes model execution, tool execution,
queueing and dependency waits. In the coding example, integration waits
for all required changes. A delay on the last unfinished branch postpones
integration, while an earlier-finishing branch may absorb the same delay
before the join. Request latency affects WCT through these dependencies.

U020 heading 
Reusable KV across agent turns.

U021 prose 
During \emph{prefill}, an LLM processes input tokens and computes
per-layer keys and values (KV); \emph{decoding} extends this state as
output tokens are generated. An agent's next request often appends model
outputs and tool results to its existing conversation. Under compatible
model and position semantics, a matching token prefix can reuse its KV,
so only the new suffix needs to be
computed~\cite{gao2024cachedattention,zheng2024sglang}.
For example, after the API agent reads a file, its next request can reuse
the preceding conversation's KV while processing the returned contents.
Prefix changes restrict reuse to the part that still matches.

U022 prose 
This state can remain useful while the agent executes a tool or waits
for another branch, even though no model request is running.
Keeping it avoids repeated prefix computation when the agent resumes.
KV is derived model state, distinct from the token history and workflow
progress stored by the application. In conventional per-token layouts,
its size grows with context length, layer count and KV-head dimensions;
a long conversation can therefore occupy much more memory as KV than
as token IDs. Sparse attention reduces the historical KV accessed by
an operation~\cite{jiang2024minference}, but state skipped by that
operation may still be needed later.

U023 heading 
Recovery and storage tiers.

U024 prose 
A serving-worker failure can interrupt requests, make GPU-resident KV
unavailable and remove serving capacity~\cite{cao2026lumen}. We consider
fail-stop worker failures with accepted token histories and workflow
progress retained outside the failed worker, and a compatible surviving
replica available. The replica has model weights but must obtain the
interrupted sequence's state. Without surviving KV, it reconstructs that
state by replaying retained tokens. A \emph{KV checkpoint} preserves an
already computed prefix: recovery retrieves that prefix and replays only
the subsequent tokens whose KV was not saved. Asynchronous checkpointing
can leave such a tail at failure~\cite{strati2024dejavu,jayakody2026ghostserve}.
A checkpoint must survive the protected failure; same-host DRAM can
survive a GPU-worker failure but not loss of the host.

U025 prose 
Hierarchical caches extend GPU HBM with host DRAM and storage to retain
more histories~\cite{gao2024cachedattention,qin2025mooncake}.
Location determines the access path: KV already in the executor's HBM
is directly available to kernels, DRAM requires host-to-device transfer,
and storage adds a read that may traverse a network. Computation and
prefetching can overlap some of this work. Retention nevertheless occupies
capacity, and copying or moving KV consumes bandwidth that may also serve
foreground requests. Discarding the last reusable KV copy releases space
but requires reconstruction if that state is needed again.

U026 heading 
Existing mechanisms.

U027 prose 
Hierarchical caching reduces repeated prefix work across
turns~\cite{gao2024cachedattention,qin2025mooncake}; KV streaming and
checkpointing reduce reconstruction after
failures~\cite{strati2024dejavu,jayakody2026ghostserve}.
Parrot exposes application dependencies for serving optimization, while
KVFlow uses upcoming agent steps to guide eviction and
prefetching~\cite{lin2024parrot,pan2025kvflow}. LUMEN coordinates
checkpoint placement and recovery routing with worker
load~\cite{cao2026lumen}. These capabilities provide a basis for
workflow-aware recovery. Allocating finite protection resources also
requires addressing two limitations of the decision criteria.

U028 heading 
Limitation 1: Request-level priorities incompletely express
workflow benefit.

U029 prose 
Recency, proximity to the next use and reconstruction cost describe when
state may be needed and how expensive it is to recover. Taken alone,
they do not determine how restoring a request sooner advances the
workflow's required outputs. An expensive history can belong to a branch
with time to spare, while a shorter recovery can delay the branch that
gates completion. Protection priorities must account for dependencies
and the branches' remaining progress.

U030 heading 
Limitation 2: Recovery speed alone omits retention value and
resource costs.

U031 prose 
A branch that can tolerate recovery delay may still reuse its history
on the next normal request. Removing its last KV copy forfeits that reuse;
moving the copy to a slower tier preserves it but adds retrieval work.
Conversely, keeping every recoverable history in fast memory consumes
space needed by other histories and foreground execution. Minimizing
recovery latency in isolation therefore does not resolve which state
to retain, where to retain it, or how much protection the serving system
can afford.

U037 heading 2.2
Motivation and Challenges

U038 prose 
We examine these limitations using agent execution traces and measured
KV readiness costs. The traces characterize branch timing; the model
measurements quantify reconstruction and reuse under a common endpoint.
Together they motivate two requirements for workflow-aware protection.

U039 heading 
Unequal progress across parallel branches.

U041 prose 
Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}(\subref{fig:motivation-workflow}) shows four
trace-derived branches. A2 finishes last, 151.6\,s after the fork;
A1 finishes at 60.4\,s. If the following Main stage needs all four outputs,
A1 can absorb 91.2\,s of additional delay before postponing the join,
whereas any delay on A2 postpones it. We call the additional delay that
a branch can absorb without postponing the required output its
\emph{slack}.

U042 prose 
The public SemiAnalysis CC/Weka trace contains 393 filtered Claude Code
sessions~\cite{semianalysis2026cctraces}. We retain overlapping subagent
groups whose branches complete and are followed by a Main request after
the last branch finishes; Main activity during the branches is allowed.
This temporal fan-in proxy retains 286 groups in 121 workflows.
For each group, we compute the longest minus the shortest branch duration,
then take each workflow's maximum gap.
Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}(\subref{fig:motivation-branch-gap}) shows a
median of 138\,s and a 90th percentile of 1,173\,s. These statistics show
substantial duration differences within the retained workflows.
The trace does not record explicit result-consumption edges, so they
neither verify all-branch joins nor measure slack at a fault. The
four-branch example is one retained group, not a representative
workflow-maximum gap.

U043 heading 
From reconstruction time to workflow delay.

U044 prose 
To quantify the consequence of a join, we measure full prefill at the
four active requests' input-size proxies. Both this experiment and the
tier comparison in Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}(\subref{fig:motivation-recompute})
use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct-1M~\cite{qwen2025qwen25oneM} on two A100 PCIe
40\,GB GPUs, with TP2, BF16 and batch one. Our instrumented SGLang
0.5.10rc0 execution uses MInference vertical/slash
attention~\cite{jiang2024minference} and dual chunk attention (DCA)
position handling, with sparse attention above 32K.
The four history-length proxies come from trace hash-block counts times
64 and range from 100,864 to 113,664 tokens. Each measured input is a
fixed synthetic history plus one token. After one complete warmup per
length, we collect three timings and report their median and min--max.
Appendix~\ref{sec:motivation-protocol} gives the complete protocol.

A1's 33.2\,s reconstruction fits within its 91.2\,s slack and leaves WCT
unchanged. A2's slightly smaller cost, 32.6\,s, is fully exposed because
its slack is zero. A3 and A4 expose 16.3 and 18.0\,s, respectively.

The costs are physical measurements; the WCT increments are conditional

fault-injected measurements of the original workflows.

U050 heading 
Challenge 1: Estimating the workflow impact of recovery.

U051 prose 
A protection decision must connect the delay it avoids to the required
workflow outputs. The example uses completed traces to obtain slack,
but an online system sees only execution so far: remaining model and
tool durations are uncertain, and later dependencies may not yet be
exposed. Priorities must change as branches progress. A worker failure
can also interrupt several branches whose recoveries share resources,
so their isolated WCT increments cannot simply be added. The challenge
is to derive useful recovery priorities from partial workflow progress
while accounting for these uncertainties and interactions.
